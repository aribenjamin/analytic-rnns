Understanding the learning dynamics of recurrent neural networks

Saddle-to-saddle dynamics underlie many ANN learning processes, and often the saddles are quite interpretable as 'modes' learned one by one. But what are these modes for recurrent systems?

§ 1

Modes are how networks learn

It is often intuited that deep networks learn "simple things first". But what a "simple" hypothesis?

We can learn exactly what a network thinks is "simple" by making precise statements about what is learned first. Since we never learn for infinite intes, these dynamical learning trajectories dictate the inductive bias of trained networks.

For example, if you train a deep linear network with a small initialization on a structured target, the loss does not glide smoothly to zero. It drops in steps.1 Each cliff is the 'aha' moment associated with the learning of a new 'mode' of the target. In deep linear networks, we can say exactly what these modes are: the singular directions of the input-output correlation matrix.2 If your task is, say, to encode natural images, the first mode will be the dominant principal component of the image dataset, and the learning dynamics of gradient descent learn low spatial frequencies first.3

The lab of Andrew Saxe recently distilled this already-beautiful picture into a more general picture applicable to other settings.2 You can picture the dynamics of learning as a staircase, where each step is a saddle point in the loss landscape. We see 'saddle-to-saddle' dynamics.4 Each mode of our learning progression is associated with a saddle in the landscape.

§ 2

What are the dynamics of learning in recurrent networks?

A neuroscientist's everyday network model has memory: a recurrent population $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ driven by a scalar input $x_t$ and read out as a scalar $y_t$,

$$h_{t+1} = W h_t + b\, x_t, \qquad y_t = c^\top h_t.$$

The saddle-to-saddle picture of learning gives us a recipe. To understand the learning dynamics of this system, we must find the saddles and the modes that emerge when a saddle is passed. So what are they?

What is the analogue of an SVD here? Your first guess may be the eigendecomposition of $W$. This is already a step up: whereas singular values are nonnegative real numbers, eigenvalues are complex numbers, and the complex plane is a much richer canvas for learning dynamics to paint on. However, eigenvalues are not quite the object we're looking for.

What we will find below is that the saddles of the loss landscape are actually interpretable as cancellations between poles and zeros of the I/O transfer function, and learning new modes correspond to new pole-zero pairs emerging from the same location in the complex plane. The rest of this post is an introduction to what that sentence means, and an exploration of how we can use it to understand the learning dynamics of recurrent networks.

§ 3

Introducing the transfer function

When we ask "what has the network learned?" we really mean: what map does it implement from the input stream $\{x_t\}$ to the output stream $\{y_t\}$? What describes the input/output behavior?

Unroll the recurrence with $h_0 = 0$ and the map appears directly: every output is a weighted sum of past inputs,

$$y_t \;=\; \sum_{s} g_s\, x_{t-s}, \qquad g_s \;=\; c^\top W^s b,$$

where the sequence $\{g_s\}_{s \ge 0}$ is the impulse response — the output the network produces from a unit kick at $t=0$. This convolution is the I/O function: hand it any input, the formula returns the output. Two parameter triples that produce the same $\{g_s\}$ are the same network in different clothes.

The impulse response has hidden structure. Diagonalising $W$ with eigenvalues $\{p_k\}_{k=1}^n$ collapses the matrix powers into scalar powers,

$$g_s \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} r_k\, p_k^{\,s},$$

one term per mode of $W$. Each mode rings as a pure exponential of decay rate $|p_k|$ and angular frequency $\arg(p_k)$, weighted by the residue $r_k = (c^\top v_k)(u_k^\top b)$, the product of how strongly the input excites mode $k$ and how strongly the readout listens to it.

What we'd really love is a clean description of the output stream that doesn't depend on an infinite series of terms going back to the beginning of time. We'd like a single finite expression that is the I/O map, with no time index left in it. It turns out there's a trick. We can sum up the contribution of mode $k$ from all timepoints in the past via a dummy variable $z^{-1}$ for each time lag: $\sum_s r_k p_k^s z^{-s}$. Because this is a geometric series, it sums in closed form — just the way $1 + r + r^2 + \cdots = 1/(1-r)$ — collapsing to a single fraction. Adding $n$ modes gives the transfer function,5

$$H(z) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{r_k}{z - p_k} \;=\; c^\top (zI - W)^{-1} b.$$

Look at the left-hand form: $H(z)$ blows up whenever $z - p_k = 0$. These singularities are called poles, and they sit exactly at the eigenvalues of $W$. Meanwhile, at certain other values of $z$ the $n$ terms cancel and $H(z) = 0$; these are the zeros (there are at most $n-1$ of them). Poles and zeros together fix $H(z)$ up to overall scale — a finite list of marked points in $\mathbb{C}$ that completely describes the I/O map.

This is the benefit of the transfer function. A finite list of marked points in $\mathbb{C}$ — at most $n$ poles and $n-1$ zeros — is a complete, basis-free summary of what the network does to any input, ever. This is the answer to "what has the network learned": a configuration of points in the complex plane.

§ 4

The residue is the readout volume knob

A pole (i.e. eigenvalue of $W$) alone is not enough to produce a resonance: it has to be read out. The residue at each pole determines how loudly that mode speaks to the output. (Think of "null modes" in feedforward networks: they are directions in which the input is projected but the output isn't read out.) When a zero sits directly on top of a pole, the residue is zero and the mode is silent — even though the eigenvalue of $W$ is still there. Pulling the zero away activates the mode; the residue grows in proportion to the pole–zero separation (linearly, for small separations).

§ 5

Cancellation is a saddle

Let's recall the big question: what are the learning dynamics of recurrent networks? We can suspect that the dynamics will still be saddle-to-saddle. But what are the saddles?

The answer is: saddles are the points in (W, b, c) parameter space where a pole and a zero cancel each other out, making the residue zero.

The reason comes from the product structure of residues. Recall from § 4 that the residue factorises as $r_k = \alpha_k \beta_k$, where $\alpha_k$ is how strongly the readout $c$ observes mode $k$, and $\beta_k$ is how strongly the input $b$ excites it. The output depends on mode $k$ only through this product:

$$\hat{y}_t \;=\; \alpha_k\,\beta_k\;\phi_k(t) \;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{j \neq k} r_j\,\phi_j(t)}_{\text{other modes}}.$$

A critical point of the full loss requires the gradient to vanish in every parameter direction — all the $\alpha_j, \beta_j$, and the pole locations $p_j$, for every mode $j$. We are not going to verify all of those at once. Instead we adopt a hunting strategy: look for points where the other modes are already at their own critical points (a one-mode-at-a-time best fit), and check whether $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ for some specific mode $k$ is also at a critical point. Anything we find this way is a critical point of the full system.

So fix the other modes at their critical-point values (whatever those are — the data picks them) and restrict to the $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ subspace. The squared-error loss becomes

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha_k,\,\beta_k) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\,\beta_k\bigr\|^2 \;+\;\text{const},$$

where $r_k^{\ast}$ stands for the target residue — a single number summarising what the data wants mode $k$ to be. (We will unpack it properly in § 6; for now read it as "the part of the target signal at mode $k$'s frequency, after subtracting everything the other modes already explain".) This loss is the recurrent analogue of what we see for feedforward networks, where the loss for mode $k$ is $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\|\sigma^{\ast} - u\,v\|^2$ for a factorised singular value.

Now check the gradients at the cancellation point $\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha_k} = -\bigl(r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\beta_k\bigr)\,\beta_k \;\xrightarrow{\;\alpha_k=\beta_k=0\;}\; 0,$$ $$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta_k} = -\bigl(r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\beta_k\bigr)\,\alpha_k \;\xrightarrow{\;\alpha_k=\beta_k=0\;}\; 0.$$

Both gradients vanish — it's a critical point. But it's not a minimum. The Hessian at the origin is

$$H \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & r_k^{\ast} \\[4pt] r_k^{\ast} & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

with eigenvalues $\pm\, r_k^{\ast}$ — one positive, one negative. The loss surface is a hyperbolic paraboloid in $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$, with the unstable direction along $\alpha_k = +\beta_k$. Learning escapes the saddle along this direction, turning on the mode.

By construction this is a critical point of the full loss (the other modes were at their critical points, and we just showed $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ are too). It is a saddle of the full loss as long as the other modes were at minima of their restricted losses, which they generically are. So the hunting strategy works: every cancellation point $r_k = 0$ — with the other modes at their best-fit values — is a saddle of the full loss landscape.

What this doesn't tell us is which saddle the dynamics actually visits, or in what order — that is the dynamical question, and we take it up next.

§ 6

Escape rates and the order of modes

We have a saddle at $\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$ for every mode. The dynamical question is: starting from a tiny perturbation, how fast does each mode escape? The fastest mode wins the race and sets the first step of the staircase.

We start from the restricted loss in $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$. Writing $\Delta_t = y_t - \sum_{j \neq k} r_j\,\phi_j(t)$ for the residual that the other modes leave behind, and expanding the squared error, the loss is

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha_k,\beta_k) \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\perp} \;-\; J_k\,\alpha_k\beta_k \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,S_k^{(x)}\,(\alpha_k\beta_k)^2,$$

where two data-dependent constants have appeared:

  • $J_k \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_t \Delta_t\,\phi_k(t)\right]$ is the cross-energy: the projection of the unexplained residual onto mode $k$'s response. It measures how much of the target this mode could still pick up. Roughly: target spectral content at mode $k$'s frequency, weighted by how strongly the input excites that frequency.
  • $S_k^{(x)} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_t |\phi_k(t)|^2\right]$ is the input energy at mode $k$: how loudly the input drives this mode under the data distribution.

The role of $r_k^{\ast}$ in § 5 was a stand-in for $J_k$ (with $S_k^{(x)}$ folded in by a choice of normalisation). Now we keep them separate.

Gradient flow gives $\dot\alpha_k = -\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\alpha_k$ and $\dot\beta_k = -\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\beta_k$:

$$\dot\alpha_k \;=\; J_k\,\beta_k \;-\; S_k^{(x)}\,\alpha_k\beta_k^{2},$$ $$\dot\beta_k \;=\; J_k\,\alpha_k \;-\; S_k^{(x)}\,\alpha_k^{2}\beta_k.$$

Near the origin the cubic terms are higher-order; the linearisation is

$$\begin{pmatrix}\dot\alpha_k \\ \dot\beta_k\end{pmatrix} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & J_k \\ J_k & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_k \\ \beta_k\end{pmatrix}.$$

The eigenvalues are $\pm J_k$, with eigenvectors $\alpha_k = +\beta_k$ (unstable, rate $+J_k$) and $\alpha_k = -\beta_k$ (stable, rate $-J_k$). A perturbation of size $\epsilon_0$ along the unstable direction grows as

$$\epsilon(\tau) \;=\; \epsilon_0\,e^{\,|J_k|\,\tau},$$

and the saturation only kicks in once $\alpha_k\beta_k$ approaches $J_k/S_k^{(x)}$ — which, looking back at the loss, is the location of the new minimum. So mode $k$ escapes its saddle at rate $|J_k|$ and settles at residue $r_k = J_k / S_k^{(x)}$.

The dwell time near the rank-$\rho$ saddle — the time from entering an $\epsilon$-ball of radius $\epsilon_0$ until the next mode's perturbation grows to size $\epsilon_{\mathrm{exit}}$ — is

$$T_{\rho} \;=\; \frac{1}{|J_{\rho+1}|}\, \log\!\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{exit}}}{\epsilon_0}.$$

So the order is set by the cross-energies $|J_k|$, and the gaps between $|J_k|$ for different modes determine how clean the staircase looks. Two modes with similar $|J_k|$ activate almost simultaneously and blur their steps; well-separated $|J_k|$ give sharp, well-spaced steps.

Two consequences worth noting:

  • The input distribution reshapes the ordering. $J_k$ folds in input energy at mode $k$'s frequency. A mode at a frequency where the input is weak has a small $J_k$ and a long dwell time, even if the target has a lot of content there. Modes the network can't hear are learned last (or never).
  • The staircase has logarithmic-in-initialisation timing. The dwell time depends on $\log(1/\epsilon_0)$: smaller initialisation means longer plateaus, not unboundedly so — the dependence is logarithmic. This is the standard saddle-to-saddle scaling.4

A subtlety we have glossed: the above linearisation lives in the $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ coordinates, with all other modal coordinates held fixed. The full system has $(W, b, c)$ as parameters, and the natural metric on this space differs from the flat metric on $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ by smooth factors involving the eigenvector geometry of $W$. These factors are $O(1)$ at generic saddles and rescale $|J_k|$ by a mode-dependent constant — they do not change the ordering or the logarithmic scaling.6

§ 7

The staircase, decoded

Train a small recurrent network on a multi-frequency target, and the loss descends in steps. Each step is one pole–zero pair separating in the complex plane. The order in which the pairs separate is set by the cross-energies $|J_k|$ from § 6 — the product of the target's spectral content at each mode's frequency and the input's energy there.

§ 8

Direction in ℂ: lines and spirals

§7 showed the staircase: each cliff in the loss is one pole–zero pair separating in the complex plane. This section zooms into a single one of those separations and asks what shape it traces.

Train an actual recurrent network with one target mode held silent at the start — a pole and a zero sitting on top of each other, a cancellation saddle. Run gradient descent and watch the zero come off the pole. For a real target pole it leaves along a straight line. For a complex target pole the residue's phase turns as it grows, and the zero swings as it separates — a spiral.

The escape is not arbitrary. The separation flow predicts that near the saddle the separation grows along a fixed direction $e^{i\arg\lambda/2}$ in $\mathbb{C}$, at rate $|\lambda|$. The widget measures $|\lambda|$ and $\arg\lambda$ from the network's own early trajectory and draws the predicted escape — the real, slightly messy training run lands on the clean prediction. The complex pole also leaves a fingerprint in time: a transient wobble in the mode's output, at frequency $|\lambda|\sin(\arg\lambda/2)$, as it settles in.

Dial the target pole from the real axis up into the complex plane, press train, and watch the separation turn from a line into a spiral.
