Understanding the learning dynamics of recurrent neural networks

Saddle-to-saddle dynamics underlie many ANN learning processes, and often the saddles are quite interpretable as 'modes' learned one by one. But what are these modes for recurrent systems?

§ 1

Modes are how networks learn

It is often intuited that deep networks learn "simple things first". But what a "simple" hypothesis?

We can learn exactly what a network thinks is "simple" by making precise statements about what is learned first. Since we never learn for infinite intes, these dynamical learning trajectories dictate the inductive bias of trained networks.

For example, if you train a deep linear network with a small initialization on a structured target, the loss does not glide smoothly to zero. It drops in steps.1 Each cliff is the 'aha' moment associated with the learning of a new 'mode' of the target. In deep linear networks, we can say exactly what these modes are: the singular directions of the input-output correlation matrix.2 If your task is, say, to encode natural images, the first mode will be the dominant principal component of the image dataset, and the learning dynamics of gradient descent learn low spatial frequencies first.3

Folks at UCL (Yedi Zhang, Andrew Saxe, and Peter Latham) recently distilled this intuitive picture even further into a general principle: saddle-to-saddle dynamics2. It's one of those ideas that's immediately intuitive. Picture the dynamics of learning as a staircase, where each step is a saddle point in the loss landscape. It's quite clear why gradient descent would get stuck at saddles, and how it would escape them one by one. Our 'modes' correspond to the thing that we pick up when we escape a saddle.

§ 2

What are the dynamics of learning in recurrent networks?

A neuroscientist's everyday network model has memory: a recurrent population $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ driven by a scalar input $x_t$ and read out as a scalar $y_t$,

$$h_{t+1} = W h_t + b\, x_t, \qquad y_t = c^\top h_t.$$

What are the 'modes' we pick up during learning? The saddle-to-saddle picture of learning gives us a recipe. To understand the learning dynamics of this system, we must find the saddles and the modes that emerge when a saddle is passed.

Let's get some incorrect answers out of the way first. Unlike in feedforward linear networks, the answer is not the singular values. If you're familiar with linear dynamical systems, your next guess might be the eigendevalues of $W$. This is already a step up: whereas singular values are nonnegative real numbers, eigenvalues are complex numbers. However, eigenvalues are not still not quite the object we're looking for. You might also guess the moments when we learn new modes correspond to bifurcations in the dynamics of $h_t$. But none of these are quite right.

What we will find below is that the saddles of the loss landscape are actually interpretable as cancellations between poles and zeros of the I/O transfer function, and learning new modes correspond to new pole-zero pairs emerging from the same location in the complex plane. The rest of this post is an introduction to what that sentence means, and an exploration of how we can use it to understand the learning dynamics of recurrent networks.

§ 3

Introducing the transfer function

When we ask "what has the network learned?" we really mean: what map does it implement from the input stream $\{x_t\}$ to the output stream $\{y_t\}$? What describes the input/output behavior?

Unroll the recurrence with $h_0 = 0$ and the map appears directly: every output is a weighted sum of past inputs,

$$y_t \;=\; \sum_{s} g_s\, x_{t-s}, \qquad g_s \;=\; c^\top W^s b,$$

where the sequence $\{g_s\}_{s \ge 0}$ is the impulse response — the output the network produces from a unit kick at $t=0$. This convolution is the I/O function: hand it any input, the formula returns the output. Two parameter triples that produce the same $\{g_s\}$ are the same network in different clothes.

The impulse response has hidden structure. Diagonalising $W$ with eigenvalues $\{p_k\}_{k=1}^n$ collapses the matrix powers into scalar powers,

$$g_s \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} r_k\, p_k^{\,s},$$

one term per mode of $W$. Each mode rings as a pure exponential of decay rate $|p_k|$ and angular frequency $\arg(p_k)$, weighted by the residue $r_k = (c^\top v_k)(u_k^\top b)$, the product of how strongly the input excites mode $k$ and how strongly the readout listens to it.

What we'd really love is a clean description of the output stream that doesn't depend on an infinite series of terms going back to the beginning of time. We'd like a single finite expression that is the I/O map, with no time index left in it. It turns out there's a trick. We can sum up the contribution of mode $k$ from all timepoints in the past via a dummy variable $z^{-1}$ for each time lag: $\sum_s r_k p_k^s z^{-s}$. Because this is a geometric series, it sums in closed form — just the way $1 + r + r^2 + \cdots = 1/(1-r)$ — collapsing to a single fraction. Adding $n$ modes gives the transfer function,5

$$H(z) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{r_k}{z - p_k} \;=\; c^\top (zI - W)^{-1} b.$$

Look at the left-hand form: $H(z)$ blows up whenever $z - p_k = 0$. These singularities are called poles, and they sit exactly at the eigenvalues of $W$. Meanwhile, at certain other values of $z$ the $n$ terms cancel and $H(z) = 0$; these are the zeros (there are at most $n-1$ of them). Poles and zeros together fix $H(z)$ up to overall scale — a finite list of marked points in $\mathbb{C}$ that completely describes the I/O map.

This is the benefit of the transfer function. A finite list of marked points in $\mathbb{C}$ — at most $n$ poles and $n-1$ zeros — is a complete, basis-free summary of what the network does to any input, ever. This is the answer to "what has the network learned": a configuration of points in the complex plane.

§ 4

The residue is the readout volume knob

A pole (i.e. eigenvalue of $W$) alone is not enough to produce a resonance: it has to be both excited and read out. The residue at each pole determines how loudly that mode gets talked to by the input and how much it speaks to the output. When a zero sits directly on top of a pole, the residue is zero and the mode is silent — even though the eigenvalue of $W$ is still there. Pulling the zero away activates the mode; the residue grows in proportion to the pole–zero separation (linearly, for small separations).

§ 5

Cancellation is a saddle

Let's recall the big question: what are the learning dynamics of recurrent networks? We can suspect that the dynamics will still be saddle-to-saddle. But what are the saddles?

The answer is: saddles are the points in (W, b, c) parameter space where a pole and a zero cancel each other out, making the residue zero.

The reason comes from the product structure of residues. Recall from § 4 that the residue factorises as $r_k = \alpha_k \beta_k$, where $\alpha_k$ is how strongly the readout $c$ observes mode $k$, and $\beta_k$ is how strongly the input $b$ excites it. The output depends on mode $k$ only through this product:

$$\hat{y}_t \;=\; \alpha_k\,\beta_k\;\phi_k(t) \;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{j \neq k} r_j\,\phi_j(t)}_{\text{other modes}},$$

where $\phi_k(t) = \sum_{s=0}^{t-1} p_k^{\,t-1-s}\, x_s$ is mode $k$'s response to the input — the input convolved with the pure exponential $p_k^{\,s}$ that mode $k$ rings at. (This is just the $r_k\,p_k^{\,s}$ term of the impulse response from § 3, applied to the input stream.)

For a moment let us restrict our attention to this one mode $k$, and consider what we should do holding the other modes fixed. Looking only at the $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ subspace, the squared-error loss for those parameters becomes

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha_k,\,\beta_k) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\,\beta_k\bigr\|^2 \;+\;\text{const},$$

where $r_k^{\ast}$ stands for the target residue — a single number summarising what the data wants mode $k$ to be. This loss is the recurrent analogue of what we see for feedforward networks, where the loss for mode $k$ is $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\|\sigma^{\ast} - u\,v\|^2$ for a factorised singular value.

Now check the gradients at the cancellation point $\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha_k} = -\bigl(r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\beta_k\bigr)\,\beta_k \;\xrightarrow{\;\alpha_k=\beta_k=0\;}\; 0,$$ $$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta_k} = -\bigl(r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\beta_k\bigr)\,\alpha_k \;\xrightarrow{\;\alpha_k=\beta_k=0\;}\; 0.$$

Both gradients vanish — it's a critical point. But it's not a minimum. The Hessian at the origin is

$$H \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -r_k^{\ast} \\[4pt] -r_k^{\ast} & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

with eigenvalues $\pm\, r_k^{\ast}$ — one positive, one negative. The loss surface is a hyperbolic paraboloid in $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$, with the unstable direction along $\alpha_k = +\beta_k$. Learning escapes the saddle along this direction, turning on the mode.

By construction this is a critical point of the full loss (the other modes were at their critical points, and we just showed $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ are too). It is a saddle of the full loss as long as the other modes were at minima of their restricted losses, which they generically are. So the hunting strategy works: every cancellation point $r_k = 0$ — with the other modes at their best-fit values — is a saddle of the full loss landscape. This isn't just an artefact of restricting to one mode: the full Hessian in $(c, b)$ space has exactly this block structure — each mode contributes a saddle direction.

Notice what happens at the origin $b = c = 0$: every residue vanishes at once, so all poles are simultaneously cancelled by zeros. This is the highest saddle — every mode is an unstable direction — and small random initialisation places the network nearby. The modes then race to escape, and the order they do so is the subject of the next section.

§ 6

Escape rates and the order of modes

We have a saddle at $\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$ for every mode. The dynamical question is: starting from a tiny perturbation, how fast does each mode escape? The fastest mode wins the race and sets the first step of the staircase.

We start from the restricted loss in $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$. Writing $\Delta_t = y_t - \sum_{j \neq k} r_j\,\phi_j(t)$ for the residual that the other modes leave behind, and expanding the squared error, the loss is

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha_k,\beta_k) \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\perp} \;-\; J_k\,\alpha_k\beta_k \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,S_k^{(x)}\,(\alpha_k\beta_k)^2,$$

where two data-dependent constants have appeared:

  • $J_k \;=\; \tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_t \Delta_t\,\phi_k(t)$ (averaged over training data) is the cross-energy: the projection of the unexplained residual onto mode $k$'s response. It measures how much of the target this mode could still pick up. Roughly: target spectral content at mode $k$'s frequency, weighted by how strongly the input excites that frequency.
  • $S_k^{(x)} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_t |\phi_k(t)|^2$ (averaged over training data) is the input energy at mode $k$: how loudly the input drives this mode under the data distribution.

The simplified loss in § 5, $\tfrac{1}{2}|r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\beta_k|^2$, is this same expression with $r_k^{\ast} = J_k/S_k^{(x)}$ and an overall factor of $S_k^{(x)}$. That factor doesn't affect the saddle structure (zeros of the gradient, sign of the Hessian), but it does affect the escape rate — which depends on $J_k$, not $r_k^{\ast}$. So we need both constants from here on.

Under gradient flow on $(c, b)$ with poles fixed, the Hessian at the saddle $(c, b) = (0, 0)$ has a block structure (derivation below Derivation. At the saddle all $\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$, so $c = \sum_k \alpha_k\,u_k = 0$ and $h_t = 0$. This makes $\partial\hat{y}_t/\partial W = 0$: the $W$-gradient vanishes, the eigenvectors are frozen, and the escape is entirely in $(c, b)$.

The $c$-gradient. $\;\dot{c} = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t \Delta_t\,h_t$ with $h_t = \sum_j \beta_j\,\phi_j(t)\,v_j$. Projecting onto mode $k$: $$\dot{\alpha}_k \;=\; \dot{c}^\top v_k \;=\; \sum_j (v_j^\top v_k)\;\beta_j \;\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_t \Delta_t\,\phi_j(t).$$ The $b$-gradient. By the same logic, using $(W^{t-1-s})^\top c = \sum_j p_j^{t-1-s}\,\alpha_j\,u_j$: $$\dot{\beta}_k \;=\; u_k^\top \dot{b} \;=\; \sum_j (u_k^\top u_j)\;\alpha_j \;\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_t \Delta_t\,\phi_j(t).$$ At the saddle ($\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$ for all $k$), both expressions vanish — it is a true critical point. The Hessian inherits the Saxe block form with cross-coupling matrix $M = -\sum_k J_k\,v_k\,u_k^\top$.

Normal $W$. The eigenvectors are orthonormal, so $M^\top M = \sum_k J_k^2\,u_k\,u_k^\top$ and the singular values are $|J_k|$ — modes decouple exactly.

Non-normal $W$. The Gram matrix $\Gamma_{jk} = v_j^\top v_k$ couples different modes in the SVD of $M$. A single isolated mode contributes $|J_k|\,\|v_k\|\,\|u_k\|$ to the escape rate — non-normality boosts learning speed. ) which is very similar to what we see for deep linear networks:

$$H \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & M \\ M^{\!\top} & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad M \;=\; -\textstyle\sum_k J_k\,v_k\,u_k^{\!\top}.$$

The escape rates from the saddle are the singular values of $M$. For normal $W$ (e.g. symmetric), the eigenvectors are orthonormal and the singular values are simply $|J_k|$ — each mode escapes independently at a rate set by its cross-energy. The mode with the largest $|J_k|$ escapes first and settles at residue $r_k = J_k / S_k^{(x)}$. The same structure recurs at each subsequent saddle with cross-energies recomputed against the updated residual.

So the order in which modes activate is set by $|J_k|$, and the gaps between cross-energies determine how clean the staircase looks. Two modes with similar $|J_k|$ escape almost simultaneously and blur their steps; well-separated $|J_k|$ give sharp, well-spaced plateaux. Because $J_k$ folds in the input energy at mode $k$'s frequency, the data distribution reshapes the ordering — a mode the network can't hear (weak input at that frequency) is learned last, regardless of how much of the target lives there.

Everything so far is a real number — the escape rate $|J_k|$ that sets the ordering. But the modes live in the complex plane, and a rate alone doesn't say which direction the zero leaves the pole. In feedforward networks this question never arises: singular values are real and escape is along a fixed axis. In $\mathbb{C}$, the zero can depart at any angle. What picks the direction? § 8 takes this up after we see the full staircase in action.

§ 7

The staircase, decoded

Train a small recurrent network on a multi-frequency target, and the loss descends in steps. Each step is one pole–zero pair separating in the complex plane. The order in which the pairs separate is set by the cross-energies $|J_k|$ from § 6 — the product of the target's spectral content at each mode's frequency and the input's energy there.

§ 8

Direction in ℂ: lines and spirals

§ 7 showed the staircase: each cliff in the loss is one pole–zero pair separating in the complex plane. This section zooms into a single one of those separations and asks what shape it traces in the complex plane.

Start a network at a cancellation saddle — pole and zero sitting on top of each other — and run gradient descent. For a real target pole, the zero departs along a straight line on the real axis: the same one-dimensional escape you see in a feedforward network. For a complex target pole, the zero curves away — its direction rotates as its distance grows. The curve isn't random. A single equation governs it.

Near the saddle, the separation $\varepsilon = p - q$ (pole minus zero) obeys

$$\frac{d\varepsilon}{d\tau} \;=\; \lambda\,\bar{\varepsilon}$$

where $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ is a data-dependent coefficient and the bar denotes complex conjugate. Why the conjugate? Each mode's output amplitude is a product of two complex parameters: $r_k = \alpha_k \cdot \beta_k$ (input coupling times output coupling). At a cancellation saddle, one factor — say $\alpha_k$ — is near zero. Gradient descent on $\alpha_k$ pushes it in a direction that depends on $\bar\beta_k$, the conjugate of its partner: differentiating a real-valued loss through a bilinear product pairs each factor with the conjugate of the other. When you translate from the $(\alpha_k,\beta_k)$ dynamics to the separation variable $\varepsilon \propto \alpha_k\beta_k$, that conjugation carries over — the leading flow term involves $\bar\varepsilon$, not $\varepsilon$. (The same product structure is what creates the saddle in the first place: the surface $\alpha\beta = 0$ is a saddle at the origin of $\mathbb{C}^2$.)

Decoupled form. Write $\lambda = |\lambda|e^{i\varphi}$ and substitute $\varepsilon = e^{i\varphi/2}(\varepsilon_1 + i\varepsilon_2)$. Then $\bar\varepsilon = e^{-i\varphi/2}(\varepsilon_1 - i\varepsilon_2)$, so $\lambda\bar\varepsilon = |\lambda|(\varepsilon_1 - i\varepsilon_2)$. Equating real and imaginary parts: $\dot\varepsilon_1 = |\lambda|\varepsilon_1$ (exponential growth) and $\dot\varepsilon_2 = -|\lambda|\varepsilon_2$ (exponential decay). The growing component points along $e^{i\varphi/2}$; the decaying component is perpendicular. The transient oscillation frequency $\omega = |\lambda|\sin(\varphi/2)$ is the angular velocity of the decaying transverse component projected onto the output. Read $\lambda\bar\varepsilon$ geometrically. Conjugation reflects $\varepsilon$ across the real axis: if $\varepsilon$ points at angle $\theta$, the reflection $\bar\varepsilon$ points at $-\theta$. Multiplying by $\lambda = |\lambda|\,e^{i\varphi}$ then scales by $|\lambda|$ and rotates by $\varphi$, landing at angle $\varphi - \theta$. So the velocity of $\varepsilon$ points at angle $\varphi - \theta$: reflect, then rotate.

For the separation to grow without turning — velocity parallel to $\varepsilon$ itself — we need $\varphi - \theta = \theta$, i.e. $\theta = \varphi/2$. The direction $e^{i\varphi/2}$ is the unique direction that maps to itself under reflect-then-rotate. Along it, the separation grows purely radially at rate $|\lambda|$. Any other initial direction gets a transverse kick that swings $\varepsilon$ toward $e^{i\varphi/2}$ as it grows — that swing is the curve you see in the widget.

What determines $\varphi = \arg\lambda$? It is the phase of the cross-correlation between the residual error — what the other modes leave unexplained — and the watched mode's sensitivity to its pole position. For a real target pole, this cross-correlation is real: $\varphi = 0$, $e^{i \cdot 0} = 1$, and the zero departs along the real axis. For a complex target pole, the other modes leave a complex-valued residual, $\varphi$ rotates away from zero, and the escape direction tilts off the real axis. The escape direction is a fingerprint of the data distribution, readable from the early training trajectory.
