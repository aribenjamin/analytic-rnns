Understanding the learning dynamics of recurrent neural networks

Saddle-to-saddle dynamics underlie many ANN learning processes, and often the saddles are quite interpretable as 'modes' learned one by one. But what are these modes for recurrent systems?

§ 1

Modes are how networks learn

It is often intuited that deep networks learn "simple things first". But what a "simple" hypothesis?

The learning dynamics of neural networks offer a wonderful lens to understand what functions neural networks are actually learning. Gradient descent follows a trajectory through parameter space, slowly moving from a random initialization towards a solution. At some finite time we truncate this trajectory, and end up with a network. We can think of this trajectory as moving from "simple" functions to "complex" ones.

What "simple" really means depends on the architecture and the data. In some cases, we can describe very precisely what the stages of learning are, and thus what definition of "simple" the network is biased toward.

For example, if you train a deep linear network with a small initialization on a structured target, the loss does not glide smoothly to zero. It drops in steps.1 Each cliff is the 'aha' moment associated with the learning of a new 'mode' of the target. In deep linear networks, we can say exactly what these modes are: the singular directions of the input-output correlation matrix.2 If your task is, say, to encode natural images, the first mode will be the dominant principal component of the image dataset, and the learning dynamics of gradient descent learn low spatial frequencies first.3

Folks at UCL (Yedi Zhang, Andrew Saxe, and Peter Latham) recently distilled this intuitive picture even further into a general principle: saddle-to-saddle dynamics2. It's one of those ideas that's immediately intuitive. Picture the dynamics of learning as a staircase, where each step is a saddle point in the loss landscape. It's quite clear why gradient descent would get stuck at saddles, and how it would escape them one by one. Our 'modes' correspond to the thing that we pick up when we escape a saddle.

§ 2

What are the dynamics of learning in recurrent networks?

A neuroscientist's everyday network model has memory: a recurrent population $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ driven by a scalar input $x_t$ and read out as a scalar $y_t$,

$$h_{t+1} = W h_t + b\, x_t, \qquad y_t = c^\top h_t.$$

What are the 'modes' we pick up during learning? The saddle-to-saddle picture of learning gives us a recipe. To understand the learning dynamics of this system, we must find the saddles and the modes that emerge when a saddle is passed.

Let's get some incorrect answers out of the way first. Unlike in feedforward linear networks, the answer is not the singular values. If you're familiar with linear dynamical systems, your next guess might be the eigendevalues of $W$. This is already a step up: whereas singular values are nonnegative real numbers, eigenvalues are complex numbers. However, eigenvalues are not still not quite the object we're looking for. You might also guess the moments when we learn new modes correspond to bifurcations in the dynamics of $h_t$. But none of these are quite right.

What we will find below is that the saddles of the loss landscape are actually interpretable as cancellations between poles and zeros of the I/O transfer function, and learning new modes correspond to new pole-zero pairs emerging from the same location in the complex plane. The rest of this post is an introduction to what that sentence means, and an exploration of how we can use it to understand the learning dynamics of recurrent networks.

§ 3

Introducing the transfer function

When we ask "what has the network learned?" we really mean: what map does it implement from the input stream $\{x_t\}$ to the output stream $\{y_t\}$? What describes the input/output behavior?

Unroll the recurrence with $h_0 = 0$ and the map appears directly: every output is a weighted sum of past inputs,

$$y_t \;=\; \sum_{s} g_s\, x_{t-s}, \qquad g_s \;=\; c^\top W^s b,$$

where the sequence $\{g_s\}_{s \ge 0}$ is the impulse response — the output the network produces from a unit kick at $t=0$. This convolution is the I/O function: hand it any input, the formula returns the output. Two parameter triples that produce the same $\{g_s\}$ are the same network in different clothes.

The impulse response has hidden structure. Diagonalising $W$ with eigenvalues $\{p_k\}_{k=1}^n$ collapses the matrix powers into scalar powers,

$$g_s \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} r_k\, p_k^{\,s},$$

one term per mode of $W$. Each mode rings as a pure exponential of decay rate $|p_k|$ and angular frequency $\arg(p_k)$, weighted by the residue $r_k = (c^\top v_k)(u_k^\top b)$, the product of how strongly the input excites mode $k$ and how strongly the readout listens to it.

What we'd really love is a clean description of the output stream that doesn't depend on an infinite series of terms going back to the beginning of time. We'd like a single finite expression that is the I/O map, with no time index left in it. It turns out there's a trick. We can sum up the contribution of mode $k$ from all timepoints in the past via a dummy variable $z^{-1}$ for each time lag: $\sum_s r_k p_k^s z^{-s}$. Because this is a geometric series, it sums in closed form — just the way $1 + r + r^2 + \cdots = 1/(1-r)$ — collapsing to a single fraction. Adding $n$ modes gives the transfer function,5

$$H(z) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{r_k}{z - p_k} \;=\; c^\top (zI - W)^{-1} b.$$

Look at the left-hand form: $H(z)$ blows up whenever $z - p_k = 0$. These singularities are called poles, and they sit exactly at the eigenvalues of $W$. Meanwhile, at certain other values of $z$ the $n$ terms cancel and $H(z) = 0$; these are the zeros (there are at most $n-1$ of them). Poles and zeros together fix $H(z)$ up to overall scale — a finite list of marked points in $\mathbb{C}$ that completely describes the I/O map.

This is the benefit of the transfer function. A finite list of marked points in $\mathbb{C}$ — at most $n$ poles and $n-1$ zeros — is a complete, basis-free summary of what the network does to any input, ever. This is the answer to "what has the network learned": a configuration of points in the complex plane.

§ 4

The residue is the readout volume knob

A pole (i.e. eigenvalue of $W$) alone is not enough to produce a resonance: it has to be both excited and read out. The residue at each pole determines how loudly that mode gets talked to by the input and how much it speaks to the output. When a zero sits directly on top of a pole, the residue is zero and the mode is silent — even though the eigenvalue of $W$ is still there. Pulling the zero away activates the mode; the residue grows in proportion to the pole–zero separation (linearly, for small separations).

§ 5

Cancellation is a saddle

Let's recall the big question: what are the learning dynamics of recurrent networks? We can suspect that the dynamics will still be saddle-to-saddle. But what are the saddles?

The answer is: saddles are the points in (W, b, c) parameter space where a pole and a zero cancel each other out, making the residue zero.

The reason comes from the product structure of residues. Recall that the residue factorises as $r_k = \alpha_k \beta_k$, where $\alpha_k$ is how strongly the readout $c$ observes mode $k$, and $\beta_k$ is how strongly the input $b$ excites it. The output depends on mode $k$ only through this product:

$$\hat{y}_t \;=\; \alpha_k\,\beta_k\;\phi_k(t) \;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{j \neq k} r_j\,\phi_j(t)}_{\text{other modes}},$$

where $\phi_k(t) = \sum_{s=0}^{t-1} p_k^{\,t-1-s}\, x_s$ is mode $k$'s response to the input — the input convolved with the pure exponential $p_k^{\,s}$ that mode $k$ rings at. (This is just the $r_k\,p_k^{\,s}$ term of the impulse response from the transfer-function section, applied to the input stream.)

For a moment let us restrict our attention to this one mode $k$, and consider what we should do holding the other modes fixed. Looking only at the $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ subspace, the squared-error loss for those parameters becomes

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha_k,\,\beta_k) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\,\beta_k\bigr\|^2 \;+\;\text{const},$$

where $r_k^{\ast}$ stands for the target residue — a single number summarising what the data wants mode $k$ to be. This loss is the recurrent analogue of what we see for feedforward networks, where the loss for mode $k$ is $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\|\sigma^{\ast} - u\,v\|^2$ for a factorised singular value.

Now check the gradients at the cancellation point $\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha_k} = -\bigl(r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\beta_k\bigr)\,\beta_k \;\xrightarrow{\;\alpha_k=\beta_k=0\;}\; 0,$$ $$\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta_k} = -\bigl(r_k^{\ast} - \alpha_k\beta_k\bigr)\,\alpha_k \;\xrightarrow{\;\alpha_k=\beta_k=0\;}\; 0.$$

Both gradients vanish — it's a critical point. But it's not a minimum. The Hessian at the origin is

$$H \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -r_k^{\ast} \\[4pt] -r_k^{\ast} & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

with eigenvalues $\pm\, r_k^{\ast}$ — one positive, one negative. The loss surface is a hyperbolic paraboloid in $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$, with the unstable direction along $\alpha_k = +\beta_k$. Learning escapes the saddle along this direction, turning on the mode.

By construction this is a critical point of the full loss (the other modes were at their critical points, and we just showed $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ are too). It is a saddle of the full loss as long as the other modes were at minima of their restricted losses, which they generically are. So the hunting strategy works: every cancellation point $r_k = 0$ — with the other modes at their best-fit values — is a saddle of the full loss landscape. This isn't just an artefact of restricting to one mode: the full Hessian in $(c, b)$ space has exactly this block structure — each mode contributes a saddle direction.

Notice what happens at the origin $b = c = 0$: every residue vanishes at once, so all poles are simultaneously cancelled by zeros. This is the highest saddle — every mode is an unstable direction — and small random initialisation places the network nearby. The modes then race to escape — and the loss descends in steps, one for each mode that breaks free.

§ 7

The bias toward simplicity

The staircase shows modes activating one by one — but which modes, in what order, and what does each plateau represent? To answer this we need to understand the escape rate at each saddle.

The feedforward analogy

In Saxe et al.'s deep linear networks, small-scale initialisation biases learning toward low-rank linear maps. The network first learns a rank-1 approximation to the target, then rank-2, and so on — each step adding one singular value's worth of complexity. Here the story is the same, with dynamical order replacing matrix rank: the network builds a low-order rational transfer function that captures as much of the target as possible with as few poles as possible.

From near-zero initialisation ($W, b, c \approx 0$), the learning dynamics visits a sequence of configurations $H_1 \to H_2 \to \cdots \to H_n$, where $H_k$ is approximately the globally-optimal $k$-pole rational approximation to the target under the data distribution. Two features distinguish this from simply activating target modes one by one:

  • The pole locations in $H_k$ are globally optimised for a $k$-pole fit. They are not subsets of the target's poles — the best 2-pole approximation to a 4-pole target places its poles at locations that may differ from any of the four target poles.
  • As $k$ increases, all pole locations readjust to accommodate the new degree of freedom. Each step is a global reconfiguration, not a local addition.

Escape rates from the Hessian

Which mode escapes next is determined by the curvature of the loss at the current saddle. In § 5 we saw that the loss in the $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ subspace near a cancellation saddle is

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha_k,\beta_k) = \mathcal{L}_\perp - \Re\bigl[\alpha_k \beta_k \bar{J}_k\bigr] + \tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_k\beta_k|^2\, S_k^{(x)} + O(|\alpha_k\beta_k|^3),$$

where $\mathcal{L}_\perp$ collects terms independent of mode $k$. Two data-dependent quantities appear:

  • The mode-$k$ input energy $S_k^{(x)} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T} \sum_t |\phi_k(t)|^2\right]$, measuring how strongly the input excites mode $k$'s frequency. Here $\phi_k(t) = \sum_{s} p_k^{s} x_{t-s}$ is the input filtered through mode $k$'s exponential kernel.
  • The cross-energy $J_k = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \overline{\phi_k(t)}\,\Delta_t\right]$, the correlation between mode $k$'s filtered input and the residual error $\Delta_t = y_t - \hat{y}_t$ at the saddle. This is what the gradient "sees" — how much the unmatched target signal projects onto mode $k$'s frequency.

The Hessian eigenvalues at $\alpha_k = 0$ are

$$\mu_\pm = \frac{|\beta_k^{(0)}|^2 S_k^{(x)}}{2} \pm \sqrt{\frac{|\beta_k^{(0)}|^4 (S_k^{(x)})^2}{4} + |J_k|^2},$$

which always has $\mu_+ > 0$ and $\mu_- < 0$ whenever the cross-energy $J_k \neq 0$ — confirming the saddle. The escape rate is controlled by $|\mu_-|$, and when we track the pole-zero separation variable $\varepsilon = p_k - q_m$ near the saddle, it obeys

$$\frac{d\varepsilon}{d\tau} = \lambda\,\varepsilon + O(|\varepsilon|^2), \qquad |\lambda| \propto E_k^{\mathrm{eff}}.$$

The mode with the largest $|\lambda|$ — the largest effective spectral energy — escapes fastest. Ordering the modes by $E_k^{\mathrm{eff}}$ gives

$$E_1^{\mathrm{eff}} > E_2^{\mathrm{eff}} > \cdots > E_n^{\mathrm{eff}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_0 < T_1 < \cdots < T_{n-1},$$

where $T_\rho$ is the dwell time at the rank-$\rho$ saddle. The dwell time itself is $T_\rho = |\lambda_{\rho+1}|^{-1} \log(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{exit}}/|\varepsilon_0|)$, which scales as $\log(1/\sigma_0)$ — smaller initialisation gives sharper plateaux — but the sequence of configurations visited is a property of the target and data, independent of $\sigma_0$.

The effective spectral energy

What is $E_k^{\mathrm{eff}}$ concretely? For the squared-error loss, it decomposes into three interpretable factors. Start from the mode-$k$ input energy. When the inputs $\{x_t\}$ are white noise, $\mathbb{E}[x_s x_{s'}] = \delta_{ss'}$, the calculation is direct:

$$S_k^{(x)} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T} \sum_t |\phi_k(t)|^2\right] = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1} |p_k|^{2s} \;\xrightarrow{T\to\infty}\; \frac{1}{1 - |p_k|^2}.$$

For non-white inputs with power spectral density $S_x(\omega)$, the mode-$k$ input energy generalises to $S_k^{(x)} \approx S_x(\arg p_k) / (1 - |p_k|^2)$: the input's spectral density evaluated at mode $k$'s frequency, divided by the geometric-series persistence factor. The effective spectral energy — defined as $E_k^{\mathrm{eff}} = |J_k|^2 / S_k^{(x)}$ — then becomes

$$\boxed{E_k^{\mathrm{eff}} \;\approx\; |r_k^{\ast}|^2 \cdot \frac{S_x(\arg\,p_k^{\ast})}{1 - |p_k^{\ast}|^2}.}$$

Three factors multiply, each with a clear meaning:

  • $|r_k^{\ast}|^2$ — target content: how much of the target's impulse response lives at mode $k$'s frequency. A mode with a small residue in the target contributes little to the error regardless of everything else.
  • $S_x(\arg p_k^{\ast})$ — input drive: how much energy the input delivers at that frequency. The input acts as a frequency-selective filter on what can be learned.
  • $1/(1 - |p_k^{\ast}|^2)$ — persistence: how many time steps the mode rings for. Slowly-decaying modes accumulate more squared error across the sum.

Data-dependent gating

The input-drive factor $S_x(\arg p_k^*)$ is the key prediction that distinguishes recurrent from feedforward learning. It can be zero. If the input has no energy at mode $k$'s frequency, then the filtered signal $\phi_k(t) = \sum_s p_k^s\, x_{t-s}$ is identically zero for all $t$ — the mode is never excited, the cross-energy $J_k$ vanishes, and the gradient in the $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ directions is exactly zero. The mode is not just slow to learn; it is invisible to gradient descent.

This is absent from the feedforward theory. In Saxe's deep linear networks, the input covariance rescales the importance of each singular value but cannot gate it to zero — every mode eventually escapes its saddle. In the recurrent setting, the input distribution acts as a hard gate on learnability, determining not just the ordering but which modes are accessible at all. A lowpass input cannot teach the network a high-frequency oscillation, no matter how long training runs.

The persistence factor

The $1/(1 - |p|^2)$ factor favoring slowly-decaying modes deserves scrutiny: is the network intrinsically biased toward slow dynamics? No. The factor is an arithmetic consequence of the loss function. The squared-error loss sums over all time lags:

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} |g_s^* - g_s|^2.$$

A mode near the unit circle ($|p| \to 1$) contributes $|r|^2 |p|^{2s}$ to each lag $s$, and the geometric series sums to $|r|^2/(1 - |p|^2)$. Modes that ring longer simply deposit more total energy into the sum — they contribute a larger share of the squared error, so gradient descent addresses them first. The bias is toward reducing the largest error contributions, and persistence inflates those contributions.

A loss function that discounted future lags — say, $\mathcal{L} = \sum_s \gamma^s |g_s^* - g_s|^2$ with $\gamma < 1$ — would replace $1/(1 - |p|^2)$ with $1/(1 - \gamma|p|^2)$, diminishing the persistence advantage. One that weighted only the first $T$ lags would cap it at $T$. The ordering of modes is always a joint property of the target, the data, and the loss — never of the dynamics alone.

§ 8

Real before complex

We've watched eigenvalues of $W$ drift during training. Look more closely at where they start: all eigenvalues begin on or near the real axis, and complex-conjugate pairs appear only later. This isn't accidental — it's forced by the structure of the gradient.

The rank-1 gradient near the saddle

The BPTT gradient of the loss with respect to $W$ is an outer-product sum:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} = \sum_t \lambda_{t+1}\, h_t^\top,$$

where $h_t = W h_{t-1} + b\, x_t$ is the hidden state and $\lambda_t$ is the adjoint (backpropagated error signal), satisfying $\lambda_t = W^\top \lambda_{t+1} + c\,\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat{y}_t}$. Near the all-zero saddle ($W \approx 0$, $b$ and $c$ small), both sequences collapse:

  • The hidden state reduces to $h_t \approx b\, x_t$ — only the direct input survives, since $W h_{t-1} \approx 0$.
  • The adjoint reduces to $\lambda_t \approx c\,\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat{y}_t}$ — only the direct readout error survives, since $W^\top \lambda_{t+1} \approx 0$.

The gradient therefore becomes approximately

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} \;\approx\; \underbrace{\left(\sum_t \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat{y}_t}\, x_t\right)}_{\text{scalar-like}} \; c\, b^\top.$$

This is a rank-1 matrix — the outer product $c\, b^\top$, scaled by the input-error correlation. A rank-1 update to a real matrix can only shift its eigenvalues along the real axis. To see why, consider a $2 \times 2$ case: if $W$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, then after a rank-1 perturbation $W \mapsto W + \eta\, u v^\top$, the new eigenvalues are roots of a real quadratic whose discriminant $\Delta = (\operatorname{tr} W')^2 - 4\det W'$ remains non-negative as long as the perturbation is small relative to the eigenvalue gap. The eigenvalues slide along $\mathbb{R}$ but don't leave it.

Eigenvalue collision

Complex eigenvalues can only appear when two real eigenvalues collide — when $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$ and the discriminant $\Delta = 0$. At this degenerate point, an infinitesimal further perturbation (now no longer constrained to rank-1, since the network has grown away from the saddle) can push $\Delta$ negative, and the eigenvalues bifurcate into a complex-conjugate pair:

$$\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{\operatorname{tr} W'}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{2} \quad\xrightarrow{\Delta < 0}\quad a \pm b\,i.$$

This is a topological necessity, not a dynamical accident. The eigenvalues of a real matrix are roots of a real characteristic polynomial, so they are either real or come in conjugate pairs. A continuous deformation of parameters cannot turn a real eigenvalue into a lone complex one — it must pass through the discriminant locus $\Sigma = \{W : \Delta(W) = 0\}$, where two eigenvalues meet.

The collision happens not at the target pole locations, but at whatever real-axis position is optimal for the current reduced-order model. Two real eigenvalues, both drawn toward a region of the spectrum where the target has a complex pair, converge and bifurcate once the gradient has accumulated enough off-diagonal structure to break the rank-1 alignment.

Why real structure comes first

Putting this together: the earliest weight updates are rank-1 and can only produce real eigenvalue motion. Complex pairs require (i) two eigenvalues to have converged to the same real value, and (ii) the gradient to have developed rank-2 structure — both of which require time. Real modes activate first simply because real eigenvalue motion is the only kind available to a rank-1 perturbation of a near-zero real matrix.

In Saxe's feedforward networks, this question never arises: singular values are always real and non-negative. In the recurrent setting, eigenvalues can become complex — but only through this collision mechanism. Real dynamical structure (exponential decay, monotone impulse responses) precedes oscillatory structure (damped sinusoids, resonant peaks) as a geometric consequence of how gradient descent builds up the weight matrix.
