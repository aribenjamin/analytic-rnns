Modes, residues, and the meromorphic geometry of recurrent learning

What replaces singular values when the network has memory?

Modes are how networks learn

Train a deep linear network on a structured target, and the loss does not glide smoothly to zero. It drops in steps. Each plateau corresponds to the network failing to capture a target direction it has not yet discovered; each cliff is the moment that direction comes online. This is the saddle-to-saddle picture of learning: the network's gradient flow is dragged toward a sequence of saddle points of the loss, and escapes each one along an unstable direction that switches on a single mode.

The right abstraction for understanding what a network is doing, in this view, is not the weights themselves but the modes: which ones are alive, which are latent, and which came online when.

Now make it recurrent

That picture was developed for feedforward networks, where the end-to-end map is a product of weight matrices and the natural decomposition is the SVD. A neuroscientist's everyday network has memory: a recurrent population $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ driven by a scalar input $x_t$ and read out as a scalar $y_t$,

h_{t+1} = W h_t + b\, x_t, \qquad y_t = c^\top h_t.

What is the analogue of an SVD here? The first guess is the eigendecomposition of $W$. The eigenvalues of $W$ already feel like the right abstraction — they tell you the network's decay constants and natural frequencies, the resonances that any input will excite. That guess is wrong, and the gap between eigenvalues and "what actually happens" is where the rest of this post lives.

Eigenvalues alone aren't enough

Two recurrent networks with identical $W$ — and therefore identical eigenvalues — can produce completely different outputs from the same input. The eigenvalue spectrum is not the right order parameter for recurrent learning. We need something that knows about $b$ and $c$ too.

Transfer functions, gently

The compact summary of the entire input–output map of our recurrent network is the transfer function,

H(z) \;=\; c^\top (zI - W)^{-1} b.

$H(z)$ is a rational function of a complex variable $z$. Its poles are the eigenvalues of $W$ — the locations in $\mathbb{C}$ where the system rings. But it also has zeros: locations in $\mathbb{C}$ where the numerator polynomial vanishes. Drag a pole or a zero in the playground below and watch the frequency response and impulse response update live.

The residue is a volume knob

A pole alone is not enough to produce a resonance: it has to be heard. The residue at each pole determines how loudly that mode speaks. When a zero sits directly on top of a pole, the residue is zero and the mode is silent — even though the eigenvalue of $W$ is still there. Pulling the zero away activates the mode; the residue grows linearly with the pole–zero separation.

Cancellation is a saddle

Now reconnect to the opening. The residue factorises as $r_k = \alpha_k \beta_k$, with $\alpha_k = c^\top v_k$ and $\beta_k = u_k^* b$ the observability and controllability coefficients (here $v_k$ and $u_k$ are the right and left eigenvectors of $W$ for the $k$-th pole). The loss, written as a function of $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ near a cancellation point, has a cross at the origin: a saddle. The recurrent staircase has the same origin as the feedforward one. We just needed to find the right coordinates.

The killer figure: the staircase, decoded

Train a small recurrent network on a multi-frequency target, and the loss descends in steps. Each step is one pole–zero pair separating in the complex plane. The order in which the pairs separate is determined by the effective spectral energy of each mode — the product of the target's spectral content and the input's spectral density at the mode's frequency.

The Saxe staircase from §1 was this same phenomenon in disguise. The mode is the unit of learning; in feedforward networks it is a singular direction, and in recurrent networks it is a pole–zero pair whose residue is climbing away from zero.
