Understanding the learning dynamics of recurrent neural networks

Saddle-to-saddle dynamics underlie many ANN learning processes, and often the saddles are quite interpretable as 'modes' learned one by one. But what are these modes for recurrent systems?

§ 1

Modes are how networks learn

A fantastic way to understand why deep neural networks work is to look at their learning dynamics -- what they learn first, and in what order. These dynamical 'preferences' dictate the functions a network learns in a given amount of time. Since we never have infinite data, the dynamics dictate the inductive bias of the final network.

It is often intuited that deep networks learn "simple things first". But what a "simple" hypothesis? We can learn exactly what these are by making precise statements about what is learned first.

For example, if you train a deep linear network with a small initialization on a structured target, the loss does not glide smoothly to zero. It drops in steps. Each cliff is the 'aha' moment associated with the learning of a new 'mode' of the target. In deep linear networks, we can say exactly what these modes are: the singular directions of the input-output correlation matrix. If your task is, say, to encode natural images, the first mode will be the dominant principal component of the image dataset, and the learning dynamics of gradient descent act learn low spatial frequencies first.

The lab of Andrew Saxe recently distilled this already-beautiful picture into a more general picture applicable to other settings. You can picture the dynamics of learning as a staircase, where each step is a saddle point in the loss landscape. We see 'saddle-to-saddle' dynamics. Each mode of our learning progression is associated with a saddle in the landscape.

§ 2

What are the dynamics of learning in recurrent networks?

That picture was developed for feedforward networks, where the end-to-end map is a product of weight matrices and the natural decomposition is the singular-value decomposition. Yet a neuroscientist's everyday network has memory: a recurrent population $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ driven by a scalar input $x_t$ and read out as a scalar $y_t$,

$$h_{t+1} = W h_t + b\, x_t, \qquad y_t = c^\top h_t.$$

What is the analogue of an SVD here? The first guess is the eigendecomposition of $W$. Its eigenvalues already feel like the right object — they tell you the network's decay rates and natural frequencies, the resonances any input will excite. That guess is wrong, and the gap between eigenvalues and "what actually happens" is where the rest of this essay lives.

§ 3

Eigenvalues alone aren't enough

Two recurrent networks with identical $W$ — and therefore identical eigenvalues — can produce completely different outputs from the same input. The eigenvalue spectrum is not the right order parameter for recurrent learning. We need something that knows about $b$ and $c$ too.

§ 4

Transfer functions, gently

The compact summary of the entire input–output map of our recurrent network is the transfer function,

$$H(z) \;=\; c^\top (zI - W)^{-1} b.$$

$H(z)$ is a rational function of a complex variable $z$. Its poles are the eigenvalues of $W$ — the locations in $\mathbb{C}$ where the system rings. But the numerator polynomial contributes its own roots, the zeros, and those zeros are what's missing from a pure eigenvalue description. Drag a pole or a zero in the playground below and watch the frequency response and impulse response update live.

The unit circle in this z-plane plays the role that the imaginary axis plays in continuous-time signal processing. A pole near the unit circle is a slowly-decaying resonance; a pole at the origin is a "memoryless" mode. Where the unit circle passes near a pole, the system rings loudly at that frequency.

§ 5

The residue is a volume knob

A pole alone is not enough to produce a resonance: it has to be heard. The residue at each pole determines how loudly that mode speaks. When a zero sits directly on top of a pole, the residue is zero and the mode is silent — even though the eigenvalue of $W$ is still there. Pulling the zero away activates the mode; the residue grows linearly with the pole–zero separation.

§ 6

Cancellation is a saddle

Now reconnect to the opening. The residue factorises as $r_k = \alpha_k \beta_k$, with $\alpha_k = c^\top v_k$ and $\beta_k = u_k^* b$ the observability and controllability coefficients ($v_k$ and $u_k$ are the right and left eigenvectors of $W$ for the $k$-th pole). The loss, written as a function of $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ near a cancellation point, has a cross at the origin: a saddle.

The recurrent staircase has the same origin as the feedforward one. We just had to find the right coordinates.

§ 7

The staircase, decoded

Train a small recurrent network on a multi-frequency target, and the loss descends in steps. Each step is one pole–zero pair separating in the complex plane. The order in which the pairs separate is determined by the effective spectral energy of each mode — the product of the target's spectral content and the input's spectral density at the mode's frequency.

The Saxe staircase from § 1 was this same phenomenon in disguise. The mode is the unit of learning; in feedforward networks it is a singular direction, and in recurrent networks it is a pole–zero pair whose residue is climbing away from zero.
