Understanding the learning dynamics of recurrent neural networks

Saddle-to-saddle dynamics underlie many ANN learning processes, and often the saddles are quite interpretable as 'modes' learned one by one. But what are these modes for recurrent systems?

§ 1

Modes are how networks learn

It is often intuited that deep networks learn "simple things first". But what a "simple" hypothesis?

We can learn exactly what a network thinks is "simple" by making precise statements about what is learned first. Since we never learn for infinite intes, these dynamical learning trajectories dictate the inductive bias of trained networks.

For example, if you train a deep linear network with a small initialization on a structured target, the loss does not glide smoothly to zero. It drops in steps.1 Each cliff is the 'aha' moment associated with the learning of a new 'mode' of the target. In deep linear networks, we can say exactly what these modes are: the singular directions of the input-output correlation matrix.2 If your task is, say, to encode natural images, the first mode will be the dominant principal component of the image dataset, and the learning dynamics of gradient descent learn low spatial frequencies first.3

The lab of Andrew Saxe recently distilled this already-beautiful picture into a more general picture applicable to other settings.2 You can picture the dynamics of learning as a staircase, where each step is a saddle point in the loss landscape. We see 'saddle-to-saddle' dynamics.4 Each mode of our learning progression is associated with a saddle in the landscape.

§ 2

What are the dynamics of learning in recurrent networks?

That picture was developed for feedforward networks, where the end-to-end map is a product of weight matrices and the natural decomposition is the singular-value decomposition. Yet a neuroscientist's everyday network has memory: a recurrent population $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ driven by a scalar input $x_t$ and read out as a scalar $y_t$,

$$h_{t+1} = W h_t + b\, x_t, \qquad y_t = c^\top h_t.$$

What is the analogue of an SVD here? The first guess is the eigendecomposition of $W$. Its eigenvalues already feel like the right object — they tell you the network's decay rates and natural frequencies, the resonances any input will excite. That guess is wrong, and the gap between eigenvalues and "what actually happens" is where the rest of this essay lives.

§ 3

Transfer functions, gently

When we ask "what has the network learned?" we really mean: what map does it implement from the input stream $\{x_t\}$ to the output stream $\{y_t\}$? The particular triple $(W, b, c)$ is one parameterisation among many — any change of basis on the hidden state yields a different triple that implements the same I/O behaviour. We want a description of that map, not of one arbitrary realisation of it.

Unroll the recurrence with $h_0 = 0$ and the map appears directly: every output is a weighted sum of past inputs,

$$y_t \;=\; \sum_{s} g_s\, x_{t-s}, \qquad g_s \;=\; c^\top W^s b,$$

where the sequence $\{g_s\}_{s \ge 0}$ is the impulse response — the output the network produces from a unit kick at $t=0$. This convolution is the I/O function: hand it any input, the formula returns the output. Two parameter triples that produce the same $\{g_s\}$ are the same network in different clothes.

The impulse response has hidden structure. Diagonalising $W$ with eigenvalues $\{p_k\}_{k=1}^n$ collapses the matrix powers into scalar powers,

$$g_s \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} r_k\, p_k^{\,s},$$

one term per mode of $W$. Each mode rings as a pure exponential of decay rate $|p_k|$ and angular frequency $\arg(p_k)$, weighted by the residue $r_k$. What determines $r_k$ is the next section's question; the punchline here is that the a-priori infinite sequence $\{g_s\}$ is completely determined by just $n$ pole–residue pairs in $\mathbb{C}$.

Packaging $\{g_s\}$ into a generating function (the $z$-transform) gives the transfer function,5

$$H(z) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{r_k}{z - p_k} \;=\; c^\top (zI - W)^{-1} b.$$

Both forms matter. The right-hand expression makes the dependence on the parameters $(W, b, c)$ explicit. The partial-fraction sum makes the modal structure explicit: $H(z)$ is a rational function of $z$ whose poles are the eigenvalues of $W$, and whose numerator polynomial $c^\top \mathrm{adj}(zI - W)\, b$ contributes its own roots — the zeros, at most $n-1$ of them. Poles and zeros together fix $H(z)$ up to overall scale.

Two readings of $H(z)$ are worth holding onto. On the unit circle, $z = e^{i\theta}$, the magnitude $|H(e^{i\theta})|$ is the network's steady-state gain at temporal frequency $\theta$ — drive it with a cosine at frequency $\theta$ and the output is a cosine at the same frequency, scaled by $|H(e^{i\theta})|$. Poles near the unit circle make peaks; zeros near it carve notches. Off the unit circle, the broader picture: a finite list of marked points in $\mathbb{C}$ — at most $n$ poles and $n-1$ zeros — is a complete, basis-free summary of what the network does to any input, ever. This is the answer to "what has the network learned": a configuration of points in the complex plane.

§ 4

The residue is a volume knob

A pole alone is not enough to produce a resonance: it has to be heard. The residue at each pole determines how loudly that mode speaks. When a zero sits directly on top of a pole, the residue is zero and the mode is silent — even though the eigenvalue of $W$ is still there. Pulling the zero away activates the mode; the residue grows in proportion to the pole–zero separation (linearly, for small separations).

§ 5

Cancellation is a saddle

Now reconnect to the opening. The residue factorises as $r_k = \alpha_k \beta_k$, with $\alpha_k = c^\top v_k$ and $\beta_k = u_k^* b$ the observability and controllability coefficients ($v_k$ and $u_k$ are the right and left eigenvectors of $W$ for the $k$-th pole). The loss, written as a function of $(\alpha_k, \beta_k)$ near a cancellation point, has a cross at the origin: a saddle.

The recurrent staircase has the same origin as the feedforward one. We just had to find the right coordinates.

§ 6

The staircase, decoded

Train a small recurrent network on a multi-frequency target, and the loss descends in steps. Each step is one pole–zero pair separating in the complex plane. The order in which the pairs separate is determined by the effective spectral energy of each mode — the product of the target's spectral content and the input's spectral density at the mode's frequency.

The Saxe staircase from § 1 was this same phenomenon in disguise. The mode is the unit of learning; in feedforward networks it is a singular direction, and in recurrent networks it is a pole–zero pair whose residue is climbing away from zero.
